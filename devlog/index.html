Orion-81

The page opens with the project at a glance and then the devlog, newest first. After it come what the game will be, the milestone road, the road ahead and the AI effort to get there, what exists today, how it is made, every test and the earlier proof of concept.

Orion-81 is a space combat game with six degrees of freedom: you move and turn freely along every axis. You pilot a single heavy ship and point its nose with the mouse. The pace is unhurried, yet the controls answer at once: every input takes effect from the next 8 millisecond step, and a big ship simply takes longer to come round.

It has a purpose beyond play. Its revenue is meant to help support software engineers and graphic designers whose work has been affected by artificial intelligence.

In development. As of 20 November 2026, 08:45 UTC, built so far: the rules core (M2), the demo's rules (M2.1), ships as machines (M2.2, done on 26 October 2026), the engine-neutral first-flight layer (M3a, done on 28 October 2026), game modes, balance and sub-system combat (M2.3, done on 31 October 2026) and air, orbit and descent (M2.4, done on 1 November 2026). By 10 November, M4a, the engine-neutral half of M4, was built on its own branch. On 18 November 2026 the first builds of the asteroid-field demo that a person can start and play were made, drawn by a small Windows program written for the project. The game engine is Unreal Engine 5.8.4, chosen on 7 November 2026; work in it began on 18 November, four builds of the demo drawn by the engine were packaged on 19 November and a fifth on 20 November. On 20 November ships drawn from their cut parts, with turrets that turn, were merged into the engine work and checked by machine. All these builds are internal.

Devlog

What happened, newest first. All dates are in 2026. The interactive progress chart shows the same work on a time line, with the road ahead as dashed estimates.

  1. 20 November

    Ships drawn from their parts, and measured frame times

    By 07:46 UTC on 20 November the next step of the engine work had been merged and checked by machine. Ships of the eight modelled classes are now drawn from their cut parts and no longer as one model each: 197 hull parts, with collars at the joints, caps on torn ends and turret models that turn with their aim. When a ship breaks up, each piece is drawn from the parts of the modules it carries. In the engine, 876 part models are imported and each is bound to its place by name. For this step the models were exported a third time with faults mended: 46 malformed end caps were rebuilt, and the heads of 14 turrets were reshaped so that they clear their bases when raised.

    Frame times were measured again on 20 November between 08:06 and 08:40 UTC, on the project's computer with no other work of the project running: the asteroid-field scene, flown by a script for 17,999 frames and drawn off screen at 3840 x 2160, at the engine's quality level 3, with its upscaler's share set at 67 out of 100. Half the frames took 8.0 ms or less and 99 in 100 took 12.6 ms or less; the milestone's targets are 11.1 ms and 16.7 ms. A short break-up scene met the first target and missed the second, at 22.8 ms. These are early records, taken on a scene lighter than the battle scene that the milestone's targets name.

    Sound was measured in the same hour over a five-minute scripted fight, on the mix the game makes and not on the sound device: the mix kept pace in each of its 18,000 steps, and its highest peak stayed 13.9 dB under full scale. One check missed: of 1,119 blast sounds that stood out of the mix, 19 started later than allowed, the worst after 55 ms against an allowance of 27.3 ms. In a test fight of 14,400 frames the engine had to leave out no effect in any frame, because effects are now thinned where they are made.

    The start screen has a new CONTROLS screen: it lists 66 key bindings in seven groups and is made from the game's own key table. One independent review of this step found no blocking fault, two major faults and five minor ones. The five minor ones were fixed. One major fault was that the sound checks could pass without measuring the sound device: the checks now say that they measure the mix only.

    As of 08:45 UTC on 20 November there were nine builds, all internal, and none is offered for download. Seven of the 19 milestones were done: a milestone closes only when Andrius has flown it himself and accepted it.

  2. 20 November

    A fifth engine build: a multiplayer lobby, and its picture judged

    By 02:30 UTC on 20 November a fifth build drawn by Unreal Engine 5.8.4 had been packaged and tagged, numbered 0.8.0. From its start screen a player can open a multiplayer menu, set up a game to host and reach a lobby with a roster, the game's settings and a chat panel. Two programs on one computer can host and join a match through these menus, and machine checks found the same game state on both sides. This is a part of M5, multiplayer on a local network.

    The build also draws the far background of the asteroid field: distant rocks, and a mining platform about 40 km away. Engine plumes, shots and hits are now drawn by the engine from effect records that the game's own code sends each frame; this was checked by measurement. The build passed all 26 of its tests inside the engine, and its packaged copy flew the scripted test flight with the same result as outside the engine.

    Before the build was tagged, five questions about its picture were put to independent reviewers: AI reviewers that had not written the code, two for each question, one of them told to look for faults, all shown large pictures drawn by the build. Four answers were yes: the far background is clear, the lobby can be read, the hull and shield read-outs can be read at two screen sizes, and the band and letters on a hull can be read. That last answer was first a no: the paint took on the hull's own colour, so the band and the letters could not be told from the hull. The fault was found in the script that makes the materials, it was fixed, and the pictures were drawn again. One answer stayed no after three drawings: a ship that breaks up is not yet seen as separate pieces drifting apart. The build's own record names this as a known fault.

    On 19 November the eight hulls were also cut into their parts by script: 197 parts, with collars and caps for the joints between them, and 32 separate models for turrets and other weapon mounts. No build draws them yet.

    As of 02:45 UTC on 20 November there were nine builds, all internal, and none is offered for download. Seven of the 19 milestones were done: a milestone closes only when Andrius has flown it himself and accepted it.

What the game will be

The first goal is the clean v0 demo: a small but complete slice of the game. It is planned to hold:

  • eight original ship hulls, each designed for Orion-81;
  • two weapons: lasers, and rockets fired in salvos;
  • waves of drones to fight, in an asteroid arena laid out from a seed, so the same seed gives the same arena;
  • the heads-up display that shows what the pilot needs to know;
  • a packaged build that runs on its own.

The demo counts as finished only when its acceptance tests and the clean-room checks pass, and Andrius has played it and accepted how it feels.

In Orion-81 a ship is a machine made of parts, not a bar of health. Damage to its edges knocks out engines, turrets and sensors one by one; only a hit to the core destroys it. Each weapon has its own job, and a pilot who manoeuvres well beats the automatic mounts.

How the estimate is made

The totals are at the top of this page. The estimate is measured from the work so far: each engine-free milestone is judged from what the same kind of work has already cost. The engine milestones have no measured basis yet, so they are judged by size and their range is wider.

What exists today

The rules core, written in C++. It covers flight; the five weapon families (lasers, rotary cannons, rockets, railguns and point defence); turrets and automatic mounts; damage that travels through each ship's graph of modules; and the world step, which can record a replay and take a fingerprint of the game state, so the same inputs always give the same result. On top of it sit the demo's rules: the sides, respawns, pickups and score, single-player waves, drones and computer pilots, and four difficulty levels. Ships are machines now: damage spreads from part to part, damage control can stop it, a pilot can aim at a ship's subsystems, wrecks break into tumbling debris that can be salvaged, and engines, shields and weapon mounts are power modules. Without any game engine, the presentation layer (M3a) adds cameras, the lens, input, the session, the host and a replay tool. Since 31 October the main line also holds M2.3: game modes, balance and sub-system combat. Since 1 November it also holds M2.4: flight in an atmosphere, linked zones with a flown descent and re-entry heating.

The rules give identical results when built with two different compilers. On the main line, 2,005 automated tests of the rules pass and 201 more are skipped, and a further 239 test the presentation layer. Each of the two test programs runs in under one second. How it is made

  • Orion-81 is directed by Andrius Bubliauskas with AI. Claude Code by Anthropic writes the code, plans the work and reviews it; Andrius makes the decisions.
  • Five written specs are the source of truth: flight, the fleet, weapons, game modes and the places. They hold the behaviour, the numbers and the acceptance tests.
  • It is a clean restart. The proof of concept began on an open-source base released under the GPL, and a licence review of that prototype found code in it derived from the base. To start again with no licensing issues, development restarted from a clean slate on 14 October 2026, with new specifications written from the game's design and new code throughout. Orion-81 shares no code with the proof of concept.
  • Work sessions were recorded on video as a devlog, with gaps, from 14 October to 1 November 2026.
  • Before a milestone starts, its plan is checked against the work before it. When it closes, independent reviews and copy scans look for outside code, and the milestone closes only when they are clear.
  • Third-party code in the game uses permissive licences only, apart from the game engine, Unreal Engine 5, and the Windows C++ runtime.
