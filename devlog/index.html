Orion-81

The page opens with the project at a glance and then the devlog, newest first. After it come what the game will be, the milestone road, the road ahead and the AI effort to get there, what exists today, how it is made, every test and the earlier proof of concept.

Orion-81 is a space combat game with six degrees of freedom: you move and turn freely along every axis. You pilot a single heavy ship and point its nose with the mouse. The pace is unhurried, yet the controls answer at once: every input takes effect from the next 8 millisecond step, and a big ship simply takes longer to come round.

It has a purpose beyond play. Its revenue is meant to help support software engineers and graphic designers whose work has been affected by artificial intelligence.

In development. Built so far: the rules core (M2), the demo's rules (M2.1), ships as machines (M2.2, done on 26 October 2026), the engine-neutral first-flight layer (M3a, done on 28 October 2026), game modes, balance and sub-system combat (M2.3, done on 31 October 2026) and air, orbit and descent (M2.4, done on 1 November 2026). The game engine is Unreal Engine 5.8.4, chosen on 7 November 2026, and engine work starts once it is installed. By 10 November, M4a, the engine-neutral half of M4, was built on its own branch, ahead of its merge into the main line.

Devlog

What happened, newest first. All dates are in 2026. The interactive progress chart shows the same work on a time line, with the road ahead as dashed estimates.

  1. 11 November

    Two of the proof of concept's ship viewers, patched

    The archived proof of concept was patched at Andrius's request: two of its 3D ship viewers now offer every ship of the page, they are bigger, and on a phone a finger turns the model at once, with no button to press first. Its other viewers, and everything else in it, are kept as they were published on 13 October 2026.

  2. 8 to 10 November

    M4a built: the demo plays without a display

    All of M4a's code is built: the engine-neutral half of M4, the clean v0 demo. It holds the model of the heads-up display, the hulls' data, the seeded rock field, and the world that runs the waves, with rocks, a target panel and the demo itself. The parts were joined on M4a's own branch and built four ways from clean: two compilers, each in a debug and a release build.

    The demo plays from start to end without a display: five waves and then two endless ones, on one world, with the same result in all four builds. Both test programs stayed within the test-time limits Andrius set.

    M4a is not yet in the main line, so seven of the 19 milestones are still done. Its close comes first: its last checks, then the merge. There is still nothing to play. That needs M3b, the first flight in Unreal Engine 5.8.4, once the engine is installed, and then M4b, the engine half of M4, which makes the demo a packaged build.

    How the work was organised on 10 November is set out in the Fable 5.2 conduct-of-work review, under the Orchestration tab.

  3. 30 October and 7 November

    The engine question settled: Unreal Engine 5.8.4

    The engine question is closed. Epic Games updated the licence of Unreal Engine on 30 October 2026, and on 7 November Andrius decided that work in Unreal Engine 5 can go ahead.

    He chose Unreal Engine 5.8.4 the same day, with one planned move to Unreal Engine 5.9 later, between milestones. The first versions aim at the highest quality possible; the plan is to lower the minimum PC the game needs later, as the project gains traction.

    The road to the first playable version is set. M3b, the first flight in the engine, gives the first build that can be flown. M4, the clean v0 demo, is the first playable version: a packaged build in Unreal Engine 5, done when its checks pass and Andrius has played it and accepted it. M4a, the engine-neutral half of M4, runs beside M3b. Engine work starts once the engine is installed; until then, work goes on in the parts that need no engine.

    Nothing has been merged into the main line since 1 November, so seven of the 19 milestones are still done. For M4a, the HUD model and view are accepted on one branch and a seeded rock field on another, and two other parts are still to come.

  4. 31 October to 2 November

    M2.3 and M2.4 done; M4a started

    M2.3, modes, balance and sub-system combat, was merged into the main line on 31 October, within the test-time limit of 70 ms that Andrius set. M2.4, air, orbit and descent, followed on 1 November as version 0.3.0-m3a+m2.3+m2.4, taking 33.6 ms of test time against his limit of 40 ms. Seven of the 19 milestones are now done: M0, M2, M2.1, M2.2, M3a, M2.3 and M2.4 (the road counts M3a and M3b as two).

    The main line now holds 2,005 passing automated tests of the rules, with 201 more skipped, and 239 of the presentation layer. Both test programs still give identical results from two different compilers, and each runs in under one second.

For M4a, the HUD model and view are built, reviewed and accepted on their own branches, a seeded rock field is built on its own branch, and two other parts have not started. None of it is in the main line, and there is still nothing to play. M4 itself still needs the engine question settled and M3b. Game work was paused at the time.

What the game will be

The first goal is the clean v0 demo: a small but complete slice of the game. It is planned to hold:

  • eight original ship hulls, each designed for Orion-81;
  • two weapons: lasers, and rockets fired in salvos;
  • waves of drones to fight, in an asteroid arena laid out from a seed, so the same seed gives the same arena;
  • the heads-up display that shows what the pilot needs to know;
  • a packaged build that runs on its own.

The demo counts as finished only when its acceptance tests and the clean-room checks pass, and Andrius has played it and accepted how it feels.

In Orion-81 a ship is a machine made of parts, not a bar of health. Damage to its edges knocks out engines, turrets and sensors one by one; only a hit to the core destroys it. Each weapon has its own job, and a pilot who manoeuvres well beats the automatic mounts.

How the estimate is made

The totals are at the top of this page. The estimate is measured from the work so far: each engine-free milestone is judged from what the same kind of work has already cost. The engine milestones have no measured basis yet, so they are judged by size and their range is wider.

What exists today

The rules core, written in C++. It covers flight; the five weapon families (lasers, rotary cannons, rockets, railguns and point defence); turrets and automatic mounts; damage that travels through each ship's graph of modules; and the world step, which can record a replay and take a fingerprint of the game state, so the same inputs always give the same result. On top of it sit the demo's rules: the sides, respawns, pickups and score, single-player waves, drones and computer pilots, and four difficulty levels. Ships are machines now: damage spreads from part to part, damage control can stop it, a pilot can aim at a ship's subsystems, wrecks break into tumbling debris that can be salvaged, and engines, shields and weapon mounts are power modules. Without any game engine, the presentation layer (M3a) adds cameras, the lens, input, the session, the host and a replay tool. Since 31 October the main line also holds M2.3: game modes, balance and sub-system combat. Since 1 November it also holds M2.4: flight in an atmosphere, linked zones with a flown descent and re-entry heating.

The rules give identical results when built with two different compilers. On the main line, 2,005 automated tests of the rules pass and 201 more are skipped, and a further 239 test the presentation layer. Each of the two test programs runs in under one second. The table under "Every test" is a snapshot of 29 October 2026 and lists the 1396 rules tests of that date.

There is nothing to play or watch yet. The first playable build is M4.

How it is made

  • Orion-81 is directed by Andrius Bubliauskas with AI. Claude Code by Anthropic writes the code, plans the work and reviews it; Andrius makes the decisions.
  • Five written specs are the source of truth: flight, the fleet, weapons, game modes and the places. They hold the behaviour, the numbers and the acceptance tests.
  • It is a clean restart. The proof of concept began on an open-source base released under the GPL, and a licence review of that prototype found code in it derived from the base. To start again with no licensing issues, development restarted from a clean slate on 14 October 2026, with new specifications written from the game's design and new code throughout. Orion-81 shares no code with the proof of concept.
  • Work sessions were recorded on video as a devlog, with gaps, from 14 October to 1 November 2026.
  • Before a milestone starts, its plan is checked against the work before it. When it closes, independent reviews and copy scans look for outside code, and the milestone closes only when they are clear.
  • Third-party code in the game uses permissive licences only, apart from the game engine, Unreal Engine 5, and the Windows C++ runtime.
