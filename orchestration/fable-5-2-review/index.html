Fable 5.2 conduct-of-work review

Four weeks of AI-run development, measured: what worked, where the effort went, and the change of roles applied on 10 November 2026.

By Claude Fable 5.2, lead planner, at the owner’s request · 10 November 2026

The question

What Andrius asked

“Review how we were working so far, see if there would be a more optimal way to work and apply that way of organising Planner, Reviewer and Coder roles.”

Measured from the tracker, the game repository and the sessions’ own records (totals only), and read in depth by an independent analyst on a sample of 18 items.

What works

What works, and stays

  1. Four builds, identical output.

    Every merge is built four ways, and the tests’ printed output must be the same in all four. It stopped the one compiler-specific defect at the merge.

  2. The clean room.

    Spec writers never see outside code, checkers and implementers stay apart, and a scan for banned words runs on specs, code and records.

  3. Independent checks of plans.

    One stopped a clean-up that would have deleted evidence.

  4. Tests that are themselves tested.

    Lines of code are removed on purpose to see whether a test notices: 26 of 26 caught on one item.

  5. Lessons become rules at once.

    The next agent already works by them.

  6. The coders.

    Unblocked, they are fast, and in the sample they were right first time on behaviour.

Finding 1

Most of the AI effort went to planning and checking, not to coding

Share of AI effort by model, in the latest full batch, 7 and 8 November

  • The coding model16 %
  • The planning and review model, as agents66 %
  • The planning and review model, as orchestrator18 %
  • Over the whole four weeks the coding model’s share was 23 %; counted in plain tokens, without price weights, the two days split about 80 % to 20 %.
  • The written record is more than twice the size of the source code (by bytes).
  • A typical work item’s record is 13.8 KB, the largest 226 KB; one change of 21 lines of code carries a record of 17.7 KB.

The coding model writes and runs code, merges and keeps records; the planning and review model plans, reviews, checks and accepts. The split is by model, because a split by task, worked out from the agents’ briefs, proved too rough to use. Effort is counted in tokens (the units of text a model reads and writes), weighted by list price so that models of different cost can be added up. It is not money spent.

Finding 2

In four sampled code reviews: weak tests, not wrong behaviour

Findings counted in the code reviews of four sampled items

  • Wrong behaviour0
  • Weak or missing testsabout 28
  • Documents that disagreeabout 12
  • Test-time costabout 9
  • Record slipsabout 6
  • Wordingabout 4

Two slips that got past people were caught by machines: a build with a second compiler and the scan for banned words. So the machine checks belong in front of the reviewers, and the reviewers belong where tests are thin and where parts meet.

Finding 3

One mind in three roles

Planning, reviewing and accepting ran on the same model, sometimes the same agent. The same planning role checked a part’s entry, ruled its own target “passed”, accepted the work and then passed three gates on it.

  • Around each coding pass, in four sampled items: 2 to 4 planning or review passes. On one item: 13 for 3.
  • One plan prescribed the code exactly, “token for token”, leaving the coder to type.

A reviewer of the same kind tends to share the blind spots of what it reviews.

Finding 4

The work waits far longer than it runs

Built in 11 minutes. Then waited 7 days for a ruling.

The ruling, about milliseconds of test time, was held back to be asked with a later figure; for part of that week no work ran at all. A planner could have ruled the same day. Across all finished items the median time from first to last record is 24 hours, and one in ten took 11 days or more; the coding inside them took minutes to a few hours.

Times come from the dates in the records; many are whole days.

An earlier speed-up review named waiting as the main cost. It was right about the waits; its changes added structure but did not remove them.

Finding 5

Technical decisions travelled too far up

One two-day batch produced 11 calls taken on the owner’s behalf and 7 more rulings sent up for confirmation. About 5 of the 11 were technical and reversible, such as when to plan, the order of work and a merge of tools. Earlier, one test-time limit was raised three times, each time by asking him.

When he answers, he takes the advice in bulk on technical points and departs from it on scope and cost: those questions are his.

Findings 6 and 7

Written many times, measured too finely

The same fact, again

  • One pair of figures appears in 10 files, 25 times on one page.
  • The word for a file’s check-sum appears 1 683 times in 235 items.
  • There was no shared command for records; agents wrote their own scripts.

Finer than the ruler

  • A rule that halts work when a figure passes its limit fired at 40.97 ms against a limit of 40 ms.
  • The same measurement’s own repeats ran from 37.8 to 43.8 ms.
  • The exact measurement needs the whole machine quiet, so it stops every other build while it runs.

The new way

Three roles, three models, three separate minds

RoleModelDoesNever does
Lead planner (one)Claude Fable 5.2owns the plan, the contracts between parts, the technical rulings, and acceptance on evidencenever builds, never reviews its own plan alone
Coder (many, in parallel)Claude Sonnet 5.5builds one package in its own copy of the code, tests first, and brings it through the gatenever re-plans, merges or judges its own work
Reviewer (fresh for each package)Claude Opus 5.5reads the change against the spec and tries to break it; also gathers facts for the planner’s rulingsnever reviews what it planned or built

The reviewer runs on a different model from both others, on purpose.

One package

The path of one work package

  1. Brief.

    One page from the lead planner.

  2. Build.

    A coder, tests first.

  3. Gate.

    Four builds, every test, identical test output, the scan for banned words. A machine decides; a failure goes back to step 2.

  4. Review.

    A fresh reviewer tries to break it.

  5. Fix rounds.

    At most two; the reviewer checks the fixes only.

  6. Accept.

    The lead planner, on the gate’s output and the verdict.

  7. Integrate.

    Merged in the planned order, gated again from clean.

A foundation part freezes the shared interfaces first, so the parts after it can be built at the same time on separate files.

How much review, and who decides

Review by risk, decisions at the right level

How much review

Records and toolsthe gate, and the planner’s read
Code inside one areaone reviewer
Code that other parts build ontwo reviewers with different lenses, at the same time
Spec text, licences, anything publicthe clean-room pipeline and the owner’s signature, as before

Who decides

The plannertechnical, reversible, inside the owner’s limits. Logged, not sent up.
The ownermoney, licences, the scope and feel of the game, anything public or final, and anything he has reserved. Asked once a day.

What is proposed

Five proposals

The owner decides on each.

  1. Make the three roles lasting.

    Add a reviewer on its own model to the project’s policy, with Fable 5.2 as lead planner.

  2. Let the planner own technical decisions,

    with a log the owner can read and overturn.

  3. Keep his limits, drop the fine policing.

    Targets are recorded; the exact measurement runs once per batch of work; a stop needs a margin at least as wide as the ruler’s own spread.

  4. One short list of questions a day,

    answered in bulk.

  5. Write each fact once.

The one-line version

Plan once. Let a machine run the gate. Review where parts meet. Decide at the right level. Write it once.

Everything measured here comes from the project’s own tracker, repository and session records.
